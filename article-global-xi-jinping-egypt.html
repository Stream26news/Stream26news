Xi Jinping Arrives in Egypt as China Seeks Bigger Role in Middle East Security

Beijing pitches a regional security framework built on non-interference

Chinese President Xi Jinping's visit to Egypt, where he was received by President Abdel Fattah el-Sisi, comes at an unusually charged moment for the Middle East — renewed US-Iran fighting, disruption around the Strait of Hormuz, and growing competition between major powers over the region's security architecture.

China has increasingly presented itself as a power capable of operating outside the traditional US-led security structure. Xi has called for a new regional security framework built on regional ownership, opposition to external interference, and political rather than military solutions to conflicts.

The timing matters economically as much as diplomatically. China depends heavily on stable international trade and energy supplies, so any prolonged disruption to the Strait of Hormuz would hurt Chinese interests directly. At the same time, Beijing has built relationships with governments that do not always share Washington's strategic priorities, making its Middle East diplomacy increasingly consequential.

Backdrop: China has spent the past decade deepening trade and infrastructure ties across the Middle East through its Belt and Road Initiative, while avoiding the military entanglements that have defined US involvement in the region. Egypt, as the most populous Arab state and a key voice in regional diplomacy, is a natural stop for Beijing to make that pitch publicly.
